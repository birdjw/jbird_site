---
title: Low-Code Has a Problem It Wasn't Built to See Coming
date: 2026-06-04
tags: [ai, automation, no-code]
summary: Low/no-code platforms were built for humans to wire systems together — and that assumption is now in tension with how AI actually interacts with software.
---

For years, Low/No Code tools have been pushed as viable tools to build custom software solutions. Drag-and-drop interfaces, prebuilt connectors, and workflow builders that promise to turn business users into developers-lite. And to be fair, they have delivered on that promise, at least in the narrow sense of getting things built faster.

But there is a problem emerging that these platforms did not account for. They were built for humans to configure systems, not for systems, or now increasingly AI, to reliably interact with them afterward. That mismatch is starting to matter in a way that is easy to overlook until you run directly into it.

Recently, I ran into a simple but revealing version of this problem while trying to automate filling out a Power Apps form I commonly have to use. On the surface, it looked like the kind of task Generative AI tools should make trivial. It was just a form with structured inputs, predictable fields, and a straightforward workflow. But if you treat it as something a machine should reliably interact with, the illusion starts to break. What you actually get under the hood is a maze of abstracted UI elements, dynamically generated identifiers, and layers of indirection that make stable automation nearly impossible. Nothing is truly stable. Nothing is truly predictable. Everything is optimized for visual coherence rather than programmatic interface.

That is where the real insight sits. No-code tools are good at letting humans build software, but they are fundamentally bad at letting software or anything non-human reliably interact with what has been built. The moment the use case shifts from a person clicking through a workflow to a system reasoning about it, the abstraction layer starts to feel less empowering and more like friction.

That friction becomes more relevant when you consider the direction things are moving in. Low-code and no-code platforms were designed in a world where humans were responsible for the entire lifecycle of a system, including building it, maintaining it, integrating it, and eventually replacing it. But we are moving into a different arena entirely, where AI does not just assist in building software but actively participates in building, modifying, and integrating systems end to end. Humans increasingly define intent and constraints, while implementation becomes something fluid and partially if not mostly delegated.

In that world, abstraction is no longer purely beneficial. It becomes double-edged. The more intuitive a system is for dragging and dropping, the more likely it is to obscure the underlying structure in ways that make it difficult for anything else, especially another system, to reason about it. That shift quietly undermines one of the original assumptions behind low-code platforms, which is that hiding complexity is always a net win.

The original advantage of low-code was straightforward. Software was expensive, slow to build, and required scarce expertise. If you can abstract that away, you can unlock a huge amount of latent demand inside organizations. That worked because the bottleneck was creation. But that bottleneck is collapsing. With modern AI tools, scaffolding is cheap, prototyping is near instant, and integration is increasingly something that can be generated or adapted on demand. Even the idea of starting from scratch is losing meaning.

So the question changes. It is no longer about how quickly you can assemble something functional. It becomes about what these platforms offer when building is no longer the hard part. The answer is less obvious than it used to be.

The real difficulty in software was never just creation. It was everything that comes after. Maintenance, inspection, debugging, evolution, integration with other systems, and now increasingly AI driven modification of existing systems. This is where many no/low-code systems begin to show strain. They tend to produce systems that are visually intuitive but structurally opaque, semantically thin, and difficult to reliably interpret from the outside. That might be fine in a world where humans are the only consumers of those systems. It becomes much more fragile when other systems need to read, modify, and extend them continuously.

I find it unlikely that low-code and no-code platforms disappear entirely. But their role may shift in a way that is still not fully understood. They may evolve into something closer to a translation layer for intent, systems that take structured human or machine intent and project it into multiple forms such as interfaces, workflows, APIs, and AI manipulable structures. The most resilient systems in that world will be the ones that treat user interfaces not as the source of truth, but as a surface expression of a deeper structured model that remains legible to both humans and machines.

That points toward a broader shift in how software itself is understood. I do not think software will be defined by how it is written, but by how easily it can be reproduced, modified, and reasoned about across different agents. Some systems will still be carefully hand built for precision. Others will be generated on demand. Many will be continuously reshaped in response to changing intent and context. The old categories of developer, analyst, and citizen developer start to blur under that pressure because the real constraint is no longer who can build software, but what kinds of systems remain coherent once they exist outside the mind of their original creator.

So the future is probably not code replacing no-code, or no-code replacing code, or even AI replacing both. It is something more subtle than any of those narratives. Software now more than ever needs to be something that is reliably reproducible and practically maintainable. Anything that cannot be cleanly described, reconstructed, or reasoned about, whether by humans or machines, starts to drift toward fragility, no matter how easy it is to drag into existence.
