There are a lot of people yelling about AI right now, and it seems few are yelling about the right things.

Some are holding up AI as a breakthrough that will fundamentally rewrite our reality from the ground up. Others fear it as a cultural and economic threat that must be resisted if only on principle. Both of these positions end up dismissing an important distinction, that some AI applications are genuinely transformative, and others are little more than expensive frivolity.

At the same time, I am fostering a rising skepticism of a class of products that seem to exist not because they solve important problems, but because they create ongoing demand for AI itself. Image generators, video generators, and music generators are drawing most of my ire in this regard. They are, in large part, toys designed to get non-technical users to engage with AI. These products are often marketed as creativity tools, but much of their role has been reduced to a PR campaign. Make it flashy, make it accessible, make it feel magical, and social acceptance will follow.

This does AI tools doing real work a disservice. 

## The question should not be “Is AI good or bad?”

I think we should be asking more about what it is **good at** and stop having it do things it is demonstrably **bad at**. Instead of matching the tool to its strengths, many people either celebrate everything with “AI” attached to it or reject AI in its entirety because **some** products are frivolous, exploitative, or culturally corrosive.

A better framework is narrower and more demanding.

AI appears to be genuinely strong at tasks like:

- reasoning through structured problems
    
- processing large amounts of information
    
- summarizing and reorganizing complexity
    
- generating technical material such as code, documentation, and systems explanations
    

Using AI tools for these cases is sensible because they align with what the technology can actually do well: pattern recognition, language manipulation, and fast iteration over formal or semi-formal tasks. 

It would then be reasonable to expect that AI ends up mostly being used by practitioners doing that kind of work. Upsettingly, this is far from our current reality, and generative AI "tools" continue to be leveraged to keep a fountain of visual slop flowing into our feeds. 

A common failure I'm seeing is using capability as a justification. If a model can generate an image, then image generation becomes an inevitable use case. If it can generate videos, then video generation is framed as progress (I do have to admit Will Smith is getting much better at eating pasta). But capability alone should not be the bar to pass. Technology can do something without that thing being valuable, responsible, or worth the resources it uses. And in almost all cases I've seen, output from models that generate visual content are not worth the immense amount of computational resources they consume.

## The strongest case for AI is also the most boring

The most defensible uses of AI are often the least visible, at least to the general layperson.

AI is at its best when its asked to work quietly behind the scenes removing friction from complex work. It can help a developer make sense of an unfamiliar codebase, support reasoning through a complex problem, and assist in organizing massive amounts of technical documentation. It can bring attention to inconsistencies that don't get caught through multiple layers of thorough review and it can speed up tedious, information-dense tasks. Those gains are easy to overlook because they are not spectacular or even all that marketed. They do not have the instant appeal that cartoon filters or text-to-video gimmicks have... to some, apparently. But they deliver something far more valuable than novelty: utility.


Programs that turn people into fantasy characters, generate tacky songs, or produce shareable gimmick content may be easy to demo, but they risk distorting the public understanding of what AI is and should be doing. They encourage people to evaluate the technology based on amusement rather than usability. It is consequential because it changes where investments flow, what gets developer's attention, and what kinds of products companies prioritize.


## What we lose when toys define the tech

When the bulk of society's encounters with AI are the junk outputs of these visual content generation models, the whole discussion gets pulled in the wrong direction. Generated images, voices, songs, clips, and personas take center stage, as if the real promise of AI was just synthetic imitation. The quieter, more impactful and meaningful uses, the ones that actually help manage complexity, struggle to compete with products optimized to draw attention. 

That is a multi-faceted loss. First, it hinders refinement. If companies chase attention through cheap demos and consumer spectacle, they may underinvest in the more important priorities of making AI more reliable, genuinely useful, and most importantly, safe. Second, it widens public skepticism, for understandable reasons. People see an endless stream of synthetic content, dubious social value, and large resource costs, and conclude that AI itself is mostly hype. Its easy to see how so many people have arrived at this conclusion. It is what happens when the most visible examples of a technology are its least defensible ones.


The standard should be simple: we use AI where it is exceptionally good and where the benefit is clear. And we definitively reject it for any uses that are undeniably trivial. 

That means asking harder questions before celebrating a new spectacle. You do not have to dismiss AI wholesale to reject large parts of the current market around it. In fact, that selective criticism may be key to the accelerated adoption of the right AI tools in the right places. It treats the technology as important enough to demand discipline in its implementation.
